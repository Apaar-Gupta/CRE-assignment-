How this differs from the Integral Method

Instead of guessing an order, integrating the rate law, and checking which guess linearizes the data, the differential method numerically estimates the rate −dCA/dt at every point directly from your data, then fits ln(−dCA/dt) = ln(k) + n·ln(CA) in a single regression. The slope of that line is the order n — so it comes out as whatever decimal value best fits your data (e.g. 1.5, 2.3), not just 0, 1, or 2.

Variable Glossary 6

Quick reference for every symbol used on this page.

tTime elapsed since the reaction started.
CAConcentration of reactant A at time t (the only data you need to supply).
−dCA/dtThe instantaneous rate of consumption of A, estimated numerically from your CA vs t data at every point.
nReaction order — fit directly as a continuous (possibly decimal) number from the slope of ln(rate) vs ln(CA).
kRate constant, recovered from the regression intercept: k = exp(intercept).
R²Goodness of fit of the ln(rate) vs ln(CA) regression — closer to 1.0 means the nth-order model fits your data better.
